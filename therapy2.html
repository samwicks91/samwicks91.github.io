Therapy? Pt.2

Given everything that happened last year, I write this with a feeling of positivity about what the rest of 2026 and 2027 will look like.

First published: 13 October 2026

[NOTE: This has some pretty unpleasant themes and I'm not sure everyone will be comfortable with what I have to say so I have hidden some stuff]

Between May 2019 and May 2024, I embarked upon three separate journeys with therapy, each in the hope of finding a space for me to feel better about the world, my place in it and myself more generally. Each led to some improvements in my life but also drawbacks, either in their approach or in the way that I approached the situation. Although for many years, after taking citalopram, I swore I would never take another antidepressant. After having talking about my experiences with SSRIs with medical professionals, I ended up changing my mind on the situation, twice. Spoiler alert, when I say never again, this time I mean it.

Now that I am in a more settled place, I felt it was probably time to unpack just exactly where I have been and where I am now, mentally. Since I couldn't make a choice between writing this post thematically or chronologically, I have decided to have my cake and eat it by having two posts with two separate themes, both looking at things chronologically. I will speak here about my experiences with actively trying to work on my mental health and then move on to speaking about dealing with my life and how that has impacted me mentally. What fun.

Anyway, if you have read the original post from 2019, madly enough posted two months before a huge event that I will come on to, they you will know the form: I'm going to go into some detail about just what has happened and how I dealt with it or just how I felt about it and how I feel about it now. Again, it may read as a sanitised version of what I might say in real life, I'm not intending this to be the case but I am looking at writing this in the most direct way that I possibly can, even if what I talk about may call for more emotive language, I will tend towards getting things across in a factual manner.

EAP

As you may or may not have read from the previous post called "A Punch", I was assaulted from behind in May 2019. A cowardly attack that left me with a broken jaw and quite a bit of blood loss. After surgery, I was recovering in high spirits. I remember being really upbeat although this was clearly a major life event that had taken a lot out of me physically. Even previous to this, I had started to feel more confident and outgoing in a way that I hadn't in some time. As I recovered, I started to notice myself staying home a lot more than I had in the past few months which was understandable as I felt like it was quite obvious that I had just had surgery and was struggling to open my mouth. To compensate for the looks, I decided to grow a beard which for about six or seven weeks.

But when I started to go out, I found myself becoming agitated and vigilant of who or what was around me and had panic attacks on a couple of occasions. I also remember my girlfriend at the time, really not getting it when I explained that I was feeling anxious and what that really meant. Unfortunately, this pattern with partners did not end there. After the situation deteriorating, and the relationship starting to sour, we broke up. In some ways, I blame the attack for the relationship not lasting but eventually my mind started to change on it, I started to feel more like this happening showed me some peoples true colours and that's not a bad thing to see. That doesn't mean that I bear any ill will towards this person, just that whatever she was going through at the time was enough that she wasn't able to be there when I was in need

It was after this break up and a subsequent holiday that I realised that I was unhappy with the way that things were going and I decided to change course. I needed outside help and I finally had an opportunity to get it. The employee assistance programme (EAP) that was available to me at the time offered eight counselling sessions to users of the service and others had mentioned that this had been helpful for them, so I made the decision to see for myself, knowing that I had been needing some help with my mental health for a long time, even before being assaulted. When I finally plucked up the courage to call, I ended up in a very small room (cupboard is more accurate) where I could have some privacy and get to grips with the questions being asked.

I had my first appointment on a Saturday and was not able to drive at the time, although I was in the process of learning so my sister dropped be off at an industrial estate where a small building housed a couple of commercial offices and an older lady who was to be my counsellor. She was sweet, and made me think that she might have at one point been a countercultural figure due to some of the interesting artwork on the walls and the clothes she would wear, certainly the kind of person I tend to warm to. In the first session, I remember talking over different bad things that had happened in the past and spoke about how certain negative thoughts and feelings take over. Specifically, I spoke a lot about the assault and how I was feeling now, and the word hypervigilance came up. It's one that I have used quite often since as it sounds exactly like the problem it is about. She then, at the end of the session, gave me a sheet of paper detailing a technique to stimulate certain brain waves which looked like giving yourself a hug. It seemed a little odd but I didn't have a rubric so I was happy to have help.

As the appointments carried on, I felt like a lot of the proposed solutions to problems were based less on getting me to challenge certain thought patterns or talking through problems that were having and effects and more tied to ideas around spirit and working on consciousness. After a few sessions, I realised that this was not what I was looking for and although I felt like this could work for somebody else, I didn't think it would work for me. For a while afterwards, I thought that this is what modern therapy looked like and that my ideas about talking through issues to reach through to the deeper, emotional truth was actually old-fashioned and possibly even a bit backwards. I decided to persist no further with the feeling like I might not be cut out for therapy, like I was somehow too messed up for it. So for the next year and a bit, I ceased looking at therapy and instead tried to find new ways to get myself to the place I wanted to be.

Mirtazapine

Two years after my previous encounter with therapeutic help, I found myself, once again, descending into a place of despair, shame and negativity. I was urged by my then partner to see my GP to try and get this sorted out as I needed a better and more durable solution than to just wallow in the miserable swamp of my mind. A solution was offered and I thought that it would be better to try it and see what I could do to make it work than to just reject it and then possibly have to deal with the tag of being obstructive and that be a possible barrier to be getting better and more positive treatment in the future.

Essentially, the conversation with my GP centred around taking a two-pronged approach: firstly, taking time away from work to deal with the multiple stressors that were causing serious problems and exacerbating the mental maelstrom and secondly, that I try a non-SSRI antidepressant medication called Mirtazapine. I was in favour of the first step of the approach as I was getting very stressed at mountain of work to deal with and the lack of sleep that caused, plus the management of staff that I was dealing with but was over and above my pay grade and often difficult due to competing demands. The second step was a problem, as I have mentioned previously, I took Citalopram in 2013 to help me with a depressive episode so severe that I was constantly unsure of whether I would be around at the end of any given week, and instead of helping with that situation, it ended up making me feel less human and eventually I got so distressed with the medication (as well as the incredibly damaging shit that occurred around that time) that I tried to end my own life.

I was assured that this medication was not an SSRI and therefore not in the same family of medications as Citalopram but instead was an NaSSA [there's a starrrrr man] and worked with different brain chemistry so that results would not be the same at all. As I said above, I wasn’t keen on being seen as obstructive in case it had a future impact so I made the decision to try Mirtazapine out. I wish I could tell you all about taking Mirtazapine and the different, weird and wonderful effects that it has on my mood, my expression and understand of self and the way that I interacted with the world but around three weeks of sleeping for between 10 and 14 hours a night and having zero energy during the day basically made me non-existent for that period. It wasn’t long before I gave them up and within a week or two, I was back at work and still doing no better.

They are with me

The year after, a lot of different situations came into being. The most important and impactful of all of these was that I was going to become a father. My mental health was already deteriorating for different reasons around this period so this added weight that was added to things meant that I needed to find a way to cope. In the end, I was on sick leave for months. None of this sorted the problems that already existed, nor did it make things much better, but the overall scope was at least paused and more harm that could have been done was prevented. Towards the end of this period, as fatherhood approached, I did as much research as I could to find out what options were available for therapy. In the end, my GP managed to get me in touch with a group called ‘We Are With You’. After an introductory phone call, I was placed on a waiting list and thought nothing of it.

Within a few weeks, I was sent an email notifying me that my case was being closed as I hadn’t responded to one of their messages. I then frantically sent messages back and forth to try and get in touch with someone to keep the case open and eventually it was. This added to the copious stresses that were all happening at the same time, in fact I had only been a father for nine days when I received that notification. A week and a half later, I was called for an assessment and it was agreed that I would benefit from ‘Psychological Therapy’ and that would be happening in the near future. This was welcome news considering that I had previously been led to believe that the waiting list was around six months long at the time.

I met with a therapist on a Thursday lunchtime via Google Meet and I was the first time that I had ever been able to explain everything that had happened up to this point and felt properly heard by a medical professional, not only that but I felt like I was being engaged in a way that was empathetic and I had no fear of judgement. It was a sobering session. I really felt things change after that hour, not necessarily instantly or dramatically but there was a shift in the way that I was starting to think about myself and the things that had led me to the place I occupied at that time. I also remember some of the fact sheets that he forwarded to be to take a look at around suicidal ideation and how to deal with it. I was immensely grateful to finally feel like things were changing positively.

I found that as I went to more and more sessions, I was able to really open up more and had a much better sense of myself and what I was experiencing, not just around my mental health, but also in life more generally. I was becoming more hopeful about what was to come. My sense of self was returning rather than every thought being of duty to the people around me. More helpful insights were being made and more useful guides and help documents were being shared with me too. I was even recommended a great documentary about men’s mental health and a series of sessions that took place at the Folsom State Penitentiary in California with really opened my eyes to the way in which men’s mental health can be approached. Sadly though, this wasn’t to last for too long as the funding for my sessions ran out, but for the time that I was able to get the help I did, it really made a massive impact on my life.

Duloxetine

Another year, another mental health crisis. This time, I really was nearing the bottom and I have to say that although there were some factors that were self-imposed, there were other things that were happening in my life at the time that really made living so much more painful and difficult than it needed to be. It all came to a head when some money that I had ringfenced ended up being spent in a way that I was not pleased with. I had a breakdown of sorts that day and had to break free of the control that I felt was causing serious problems in my life at the time.

The problem is, that breakdown scared the hell out of other people in my life at the time and they, rightly, stated that I needed help. Once again, I made some calls and spoke to my GP. This time I was prioritised due to being the father of a young child and was pushed towards the front of the queue. After a few days, I was sat in an NHS meeting room speaking to a practitioner about what was going on, the help that I had received in the past and the reasons that led me to this point. The answer was, you guessed it, more medication! By this point, I was really not looking to take any more medication and I recall saying as such to the practitioner. They convinced me that these were like neither of the types of antidepressants that I had taken before and that the side effects would not be as cumbersome at they had been with those two. With some trepidation, I accepted the prescription for Duloxetine and began taking the medication.

Of the three medications that I took for my mental health, this was the one that I used for the longest amount of time by a massive amount. I took Duloxetine daily for sixteen months. Firstly, I want to talk about why that is and then I’ll get into the drawbacks and the reason that I ended up quitting. Duloxetine started off with no major drawbacks compared to the two previous medications I had taken. With them there was first the feeling of turning into a zombie and having little to no emotion whatsoever and with the latter, there wasn’t time to have any emotions because I was spending the majority of my time in bed either asleep or too tired to get up. The only side effect that I had with Duloxetine at first was restless legs, which was seriously testing when I wanted to sleep but absolutely a price worth paying.

Then came the good parts of taking Duloxetine. I noticed quite quickly how much more fun I had and how much less things were getting to me after taking them. I went from being miserable to being somewhat happy and even at times joyful. This can’t be taken for granted when I have spent the vast majority of my life with a background radiation level of suicidal thoughts. I was actually free of these for the first time in my life since I was probably eleven. Then, and this was probably the biggest revelation to me, was that my self-confidence went from non-existent to self-respectful in months. I have never felt a change like it in my life and I was certain that I would be taking the medication for the rest of my life.

So why on Earth would I stop taking something that beneficial? Hold your horses. With a lot of these things, just because they start out all fairytales and rainbows, doesn’t mean that they remain so sunny. Certain… anatomical, libidinal changes had occurred due to the medication having certain impacts, but that only started to become apparent after six months of taking the medication and by no means was that a negative for anyone who might have enjoyed… sharing such experiences (although I find going for hours to be tedious if I’m totally honest). Eventually though, this benefit for any partner I had at the time began to feed a vicious cycle where things would cease to be enjoyable so I wouldn’t be as receptive to certain advances and then I lost a lot of interest in that sort of thing and that’s a red flag for me! In retrospect, I don’t think it was entirely medically induced. It turns out that if you feel a certain level of stress or anxiety about your partner and then that stress is removed by not feeling responsible for that part of your life, it can allow you to enjoy your life again.

I also started to lose the confidence boost that was being generated from taking the medication and I think that the impact was starting to wear off in all the positive was whilst the negatives, mainly headaches and the hideously restless legs, the tipping point had been reached and in fact surpassed. So, after fourteen months, I went to my GP and asked them to help with planning my withdrawal. During the initial part of the consultation, I mentioned wanting to wean off of Duloxetine and she immediately looked back at me and said “well I can totally understand why for a man of your age”. Words fail me. The six-week withdrawal period was tough and to be honest, rushed in a bid to get off of these drugs but it had to be done.

It was a brutal six weeks. The restless legs and headaches were soon joined by persistent brain zaps that would instantly become debilitating if I tried to turn my head too fast, and by too fast I mean incredibly slowly. I was also irritable and a bit of a struggle to be around at times although there were certainly other things going on that did not help the situation, especially considering the lack of support I had around me at the time. After discussing with my partner at the time, I decided to swap the drugs for therapy once more, especially after the benefit that I saw from previous time I had such help.

A Novel "Mindful" Approach

Having looked around at a few different places to see if there were any therapists around that looked good and weren't going to try to get me to go down the usual CBT rabbit hole, I settled on someone in town. After first meeting him in his office, I immediately felt at ease, he was intelligent and engaging without being overly loquacious. His focus appeared to be more on the brain and how it functions and the way in which controlling those processes would have beneficial effects. It felt very similar to the work that I did with computers in some ways, i.e. find the faulty process or service and fix that piece of malfunctioning software.

This was the first of two thoughts that ballooned to the point that I stopped taking this form of therapy seriously. As I say, at first it seemed to be a great idea to me but as I started thinking to myself, I got to the point where I felt like this was all too simplistic and much like trope from a cheap sci-fi paperback, making inaccurate comparisons between computers and people. The second thought that came to my head, perhaps because the first challenge to these ideas had created the space to question more aggressively, came when he lifted a large binder of documents, leafed through them to find the right section, removed a sheet from a wallet and handed me a piece of paper with homework. The idea of having homework to take away and deal with wasn't exactly a problem, in fact I felt like it was par for the course even if previously this was more nebulous than a worksheet of sorts. The problem was the form. To hand me something like that felt very "one-size-fits-all" and didn't site right with me. I after a couple of examples of this happening, I decided to leave it and move on.

For a few months, I had nothing at all in terms of therapy or medication. Luckily, I would be signposted in the right direction and ended up paying for a therapist nearby who I have been seeing for over a year now. Succinctly, I'm better mentally now than I think I ever have been and it's a testament to the work many of the people mentioned above, but in particular my current therapist who has been fantastic. I don't really want to go into too much detail about this process because it is ongoing but I'm pleased to feel like I'm heading in the right direction.
